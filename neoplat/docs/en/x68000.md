# The Sharp X68000 inside (and what NeoPlat does with it)

This is what you need to know about the hardware to make sense of the generated
code, and what it cost to find out, which here was nearly everything.

## The computer in four lines

| | |
|---|---|
| CPU | Motorola 68000 at 10 MHz — the same family as the other six |
| Video | CYNTHIA: 128 sprites of 16×16 and background layers from the same chip |
| Colour | 16-bit GRBi word: 65,536, and 16 per palette block |
| Patterns | 32 KB of PCG at `$EB8000`, 128 bytes per 16×16 graphic |
| Text | a separate plane at `$E00000`, 1024×1024 and four bitplanes |
| Sound | YM2151 (eight FM channels) and an MSM6258 ADPCM |
| System | Human68k, with the ROM (IPL) and its IOCS calls |

Of the kit's machines this is the one that gives the 68000 the least work, and
for one specific reason: **the patterns are 16×16**, exactly NeoPlat's tile
size. On the Mega Drive each tile has to be split into four 8×8 ones and on the
Neo Geo the stage is drawn with columns of sprites; here a level cell is a
name-table cell and that is that.

## What came out of measuring it, not reading about it

This machine was ported **by trying things in the emulator**, not by copying
registers out of a manual. The first version of the code was written from
scattered documentation, compiled, booted… and drew nothing. A probe was
written: a program that turns bits on, draws something and writes on screen
which values it is trying, so that the result can be read off a screenshot.
This is what it said:

- **The CRTC's `R00`–`R07` registers are write-only**: they read back as zero.
  So Sharp's good numbers cannot be copied by reading them back. What the
  startup does is ask the ROM to set a mode (`_CRTMOD`) and write its own
  timing over the top.
- **The whole chip is turned on with bit 9 of `$EB0808`.** Without that bit
  there are no sprites and no layer, whatever the other registers say.
- **The patterns are 16×16 only if bit 0 of `$EB0810` is one.** At zero they
  are 8×8, and a name table made for 16×16 comes out diced.
- **`_SP_INIT` does not always return.** The startup called both of the ROM's
  sprite routines (`_SP_INIT`, $B0, and `_SP_ON`, $B1) as well as `_CRTMOD`.
  Depending on which Human68k the game was launched from, `_SP_INIT` **does not
  return**: the startup hangs there forever, with a black screen in the ROM's
  mode, never getting as far as writing its own timing. From outside it looks
  like the game does not start. It was found by painting a mark on the graphics
  screen for each step of the startup and counting from the emulator how many
  lines came out: the one before `_SP_INIT` appeared and none of the ones
  after. They are not needed: the engine turns the chip on with bit 9 of
  `BG_CTRL` and uploads the patterns and palettes itself.
- **The graphics screen's scroll is the `R12`/`R13` pair, and only that one.**
  The CRTC has four pairs (`R12`–`R19`), one per page, and at 16 colours the
  visible one is page 0. Writing all four with the same value —which seems
  reasonable if you think the four pages are the four planes— **moves
  nothing**: with `R12` at 200 the roadway shifts 200 pixels, and also writing
  `R14`, `R16` and `R18` leaves it nailed down.
- **The name table lives inside the PCG itself**: `$EBC000` and `$EBE000` are
  patterns 128–191 and 192–255. Whoever uses a background layer loses them, and
  that is why the real limit is **192 patterns** and not 256.
- **A pattern's four quadrants go by columns**, not in reading order:
  top-left, bottom-left, top-right, bottom-right. In reading order the stage
  showed each tile twice at half height and the actors came out in chunks.
- **The text plane reads from the sprite palette**, it has none of its own.
  That is why colour 15 of the first block is reserved for the scoreboard, just
  like the last colour on the Atari ST and the Amiga.
- **Only one background layer could be got to show.** The chip has two, but
  with both turned on, each reading its own table and with **different graphics
  in each** (red on the left in one, green on the right in the other), the two
  were never seen at the same time, in any combination of `$EB0808` and
  `$EB0810`. So the stage keeps the layer.
- **The parallax goes on the graphics screen**, which is another chip: it is
  seen at the same time as the layer, behind it, and has hardware scroll of its
  own. Measured with the same probe: painting bands in GVRAM and turning the
  layer on, both are seen, and moving `$E80018` the graphics screen shifts by
  itself. In the kit's screen mode it is **a single page** 512 pixels wide that
  repeats, so one layer is drawn per level (the furthest) and the compiler
  warns if the game has more.

## How NeoPlat draws

```
graphics   the parallax layer, with its own scroll (GVRAM, $C00000)
BG layer   the stage, with hardware scroll
sprites    the actors (players, enemies, objects, shots)
text       the scoreboard, which this way costs neither a pattern nor a sprite
```

- **The stage** is the name table, 64×64 cells, which repeats by itself. A
  level longer than 64 cells is drawn column by column as the camera advances,
  the same as on the Mega Drive.
- **Scrolling is free**: two registers (`$EB0800` and `$EB0802`) say where the
  map starts being read from.
- **The actors are not drawn**: each 16×16 piece is a sprite and the chip
  composes it. With 128 sprites and one-tile actors there is plenty to spare.
- **The scoreboard** goes on the text plane, which is drawn over everything and
  spends none of what is scarce.
- **The parallax** is an image in GVRAM, not tiles: it is written once at the
  start of the level, repeated until the page's 512 pixels are full, and from
  then on the scroll costs **two registers per frame** and spends not one PCG
  pattern. The emulator test measures it: running right, the ground shifts 67
  pixels and the sky 14, that is 0.21 of the scroll, which is exactly that
  layer's `velocidad: 0.2` in the `game.yaml`.

## The screen: a real 320 × 224

The kit's other machines show 320×224 and so does this one, with nothing
cropped. The CRTC is asked for that size instead of accepting a catalogue mode,
and the sprite chip takes it: it is checked in the emulator, with the layer
filling all 320×224 and the sprites landing where they should. That was the
reasonable doubt —this machine normally works at 256 points— and the answer was
that the narrow window that was feared is not needed.

## Graphics format

**Colour**: a 16-bit word `GGGGG RRRRR BBBBB I`. Five bits per channel and an
intensity bit at the end which is the **LSB shared by all three**. That is why
each channel cannot be rounded on its own and then voted on: the converter
tries both values of the bit and keeps whichever is least far off in total.

**Patterns**: 16×16 in 128 bytes, four 8×8 quadrants **by columns**, two pixels
per byte and the high nibble on the left. Colour 0 of each block is the
transparent one.

**Palettes**: 16 blocks of 16 colours at `$E82200`, shared by the sprites and
the layer. Colour 15 of block 0 is the scoreboard's.

## The executable and the floppy

**The executable** is a Human68k `.X`: a 64-byte header (`HU\0\0`, text, data
and BSS sizes) and a delta **relocation table**, because the system loads the
program wherever it fits. `tools/ngplat/x68k.py` writes it from the ELF.

**The floppy** is a FAT12 **with 1024-byte sectors**: 77 tracks × 2 sides × 8
sectors. The FAT and the directory are the usual ones, but the boot sector is
not: the manufacturer's name takes 16 bytes and the BPB goes at `0x12` and in
**big endian**. This came out of looking at a real Sharp system disk, not out
of documentation; the first version copied the BPB from a tool that **makes**
them (at `0x0B` and little endian) so that the disk could be read with a PC
library, and it was wrong.

**The floppy the kit generates does not boot on its own, and that is not a
bug.** Human68k is Sharp's software and cannot be distributed, so the `.xdf`
carries the game for you to copy onto a system disk:

```
copy A:JUEGO.X B:
```

or, if you would rather it booted by itself, put the `.X` on your Human68k disk
and add to its `AUTOEXEC.BAT`:

```
A:\JUEGO.X
```

## Trying it in an emulator

The test `tests/emulador_x68000.py` does exactly that: it takes your Human68k
disk, puts the `.X` on it, changes its `AUTOEXEC.BAT` and boots it in px68k
while watching the screen. It needs three things that are not ours:

| | |
|---|---|
| core | `px68k_libretro.so` (or `NEOPLAT_CORE_X68000`) |
| ROMs | `iplrom.dat` and `cgrom.dat` in `<system>/keropi/` (or `NEOPLAT_X68000_ROMS`) |
| system | a bootable Human68k floppy in `NEOPLAT_HUMAN68K` |

If any is missing, the test skips itself.

One emulator detail that cost a good while: **px68k does not advance its
internal time with `retro_run`**. It carries it on `SET_FRAME_TIME_CALLBACK`,
and until the kit's test bench answered that call, the ROM booted but the
floppy never turned: the startup text appeared and there it stayed. It was not
the ROMs, nor the disk format, nor the core's options.

## The controllers

An 8255 at `$E9A000`: **port A** is the first player's controller and **port
B** the second's. They are MSX-style pads, with pull-up resistors: a bit **at
zero** is a pressed button. They only have two buttons, so the jump button also
acts as START, just as on the Atari ST.

## The sound: the YM2151

Eight FM channels with four operators each, and the 68000 drives them directly:
there is no sound CPU in between like the Neo Geo's or the Mega Drive's Z80.
The split is the one used on the other machines:

```
channel 0   the melody
channel 1   the accompaniment
channel 7   the effects
```

The effects go on 7 and not on 2 for a reason: **the chip's noise generator
only comes out of the last operator of channel 7**, and the effects are the
only steps that can ask for noise.

**The voice comes from the Mega Drive, as it is.** What each track sounds like
is written once in the `game.yaml` (`timbres:`, see
[sonido.md](sonido.md#what-it-sounds-like-timbres)) and the kit emits **the
same bytes** for the YM2151 as for the YM2612: both store the same six numbers
per operator in the same shape, and even in the same odd order (1, 3, 2, 4).
The only thing that changes is where they go —the OPM uses `$40`, `$60`, `$80`,
`$A0`, `$C0` and `$E0` where the OPN uses `$30`..`$80`— and how far apart: the
operators go four at a time on the OPN and eight at a time here. The algorithm
and feedback register also matches in its bottom six bits; the OPM adds the two
speakers on top.

Before this the four operators sounded in parallel with a clean sine, which was
all there was: the chip played the right notes with no voice at all.

**The note table was hard.** This chip does not take a period, it takes a note:
a *key code* with three bits of octave and four of note, and of every four note
codes only three are valid. The table that goes around is not the one the chip
makes, and measuring it is harder than it looks: measuring single notes or in
stretches produced **three different tables, all three wrong**, because the
capture slips by one note and you do not notice.

What worked was looking at it from inside the game: the driver wrote on the
scoreboard, bit by bit, the code it was sending the chip, and each code was
paired with the frequency coming out of the speaker. With seven pairs it was
clear that everything was sounding **16 semitones too high**, and the right sum
came out of that: nine is added to the semitone, it is spread three in every
four, and anything over fifteen carries into the octave. Like this:

| | |
|---|---|
| C | 12 |
| C# | 13 |
| D | 14 |
| D# to B | 0, 1, 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, already in the next octave's block |

And the octave field goes **one below** the musical octave: C#5 is key code
`$4D`.

The rest of what had to be learned:

- **The chip has to be silenced channel by channel** before touching anything.
  Setting all its registers to zero leaves TL at 0, which on this chip is
  **maximum** volume: if some channel was sounding, it bellows over the music.
- **Between the register number and the value you have to wait**, and the pair
  cannot be split. That is why the game switches to supervisor mode and closes
  the interrupts while it writes.
- **A rest comes with the note at zero** and has to be treated as a rest:
  playing it would give the chip's lowest C, which in FM weighs more than the
  melody and eats it.
- **The accompaniment goes four points below the melody.** On a square wave the
  two sound alike; in FM the low note weighs far more.

The instrument is deliberately the simplest thing that sounds: algorithm 7, the
four operators in parallel, multiplier 1 and no decay envelope. It gives a
clean tone, which is what is needed; a real patch can be put in later without
touching anything else.

The emulator test checks that **the 16 notes of the `game.yaml`'s melody**
sound, just as on the other six machines, and that on a jump the effect is
heard over the background.

### Digital samples

The other sound chip is an **MSM6258**, and it does not read raw bytes: it
reads **half a byte per sample**, in the OKI family's ADPCM —the same one the
Neo Geo already uses for its YM2610, so the kit's encoder works as it is—. A
sample takes half as much here as on the other machines.

The 68000 does not play it by hand: the ROM is told where the data is
(`_ADPCMOUT`) and it puts it out over DMA while the game gets on with its own
business.

The rate is measured too, and at the second attempt. The first time a **3000 Hz**
tone was encoded and played with each mode: the usual staircase came out —3.9 /
5.2 / 7.8 / 10.4 kHz at `$0003`, `$0103`, `$0203` and `$0303`— and the fifth,
which in theory is 15.6 kHz, seemed not to sound. It was the measurement that
was wrong: a 3000 Hz tone **does not fit** at a 3.9 kHz sampling rate (there the
maximum is 1950), so at the low rates what was heard was an alias, and the
fifth mode was judged with the wrong yardstick.

Repeated with a **1000 Hz** tone, which fits in all of them, all five modes
sound and the staircase comes out whole:

| mode | rate |
|---|---|
| `$0003` | 3.9 kHz |
| `$0103` | 5.2 kHz |
| `$0203` | 7.8 kHz |
| `$0303` | 10.4 kHz |
| `$0403` | **15.6 kHz** |

The kit uses the fastest, `$0403`, which is the best this machine gives. It
shows: with the same test effect (a pure 3000 Hz tone), on a jump the 3000 Hz
band rises 1556 times over the background, against 892 when it ran at 10.4.

## The road: one interrupt per segment

In a driving game (`vista: carretera`) the stage is **not drawn**: in that view
the map is the course, not what you see. The BG layer is turned off entirely
—the chip stays on, since the sprites come out of it— and what you see is the
roadway on the graphics screen, with the traffic and the car as sprites on top.

The roadway image is the same one the Mega Drive gets: 512 wide, in
perspective, with the four bands drawn inside it. It is painted once on
entering the level, like any layer.

What this machine does not have is a per-line scroll table: it has **one**
register for the whole graphics screen. And with a corner that will not do.
Measured in the engine with the scaffold's circuit:

| screen line | where the roadway's axis falls |
|---|---|
| 90 (the horizon) | 160 |
| 154 | 233 |
| 218 | 331 |
| 223 (where the car is) | 340 |

Anchoring the scroll on a single line leaves the car off the asphalt, and that
shows. So the roadway is slid from **the raster interrupt**: the CRTC warns
when the beam reaches the line given in `R09`, and that warning comes into the
MFP through `GPIP6` (channel 14, vector `$4E`). Each jump writes its segment's
scroll and leaves `R09` on the next one's line.

224 jumps are not needed: the corner means many consecutive lines shift by the
same amount, so the frame is split into **segments** —one entry per change— and
a few dozen come out. It is the same sum the Jaguar does with its object list.

The table is double: the game fills one while the interrupt reads the other,
and they are swapped in the vertical blank. If the one being read were written
to, the beam would catch half a segment of the previous frame and half of the
new one.

## What is missing

- **More than one parallax layer per level.** The graphics screen is a single
  page in this mode, so the furthest one is drawn. With `amiga: 8colores` the
  same happens on the Amiga: that is how it is.
- **Longer samples.** Each effect has 32 KB of ADPCM (64,000 samples), which at
  15.6 kHz is four seconds. For more you would have to read from disk while it
  sounds.
